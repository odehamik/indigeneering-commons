40\. NÄNABUSHU IS MIRACULOUSLY FED BEAR-GREASE.¹

Nänabushu Is Miraculously Fed Bear-Grease (2)

<span class="l-eng">*Narrated by probably Wâsagunäckạnk (Michelson’s own hedge, carried) — Series 5 (Nos. 40–42), Bois Fort (per Michelson’s roster, Part I Preface p. XX; not credited on the story page itself).*</span>

<span class="l-orig">(1) Nänabucu a∙ī∙ndā wīwạn kayä mīnawā kayä ‘iᵉi´ᵘ unītcānisaᵉ nīciwaᵉ. Mīsa´ mī∙i´∙ᵘ wī‘pibōnicit Nänabucu, kāwin kägō udayāsīn kämīdcit. <span class="l-fiero">**Nanabosho a’iindaa wiiwan gaye miinawaa gaye i’iw oniijaanisag niizhiwag. Miisa mii’iw wii-biiboonizhid Nanabosho, gaawiin gego odayaasiin gaa-miijid. Ningoding oganoonigoon wiiwan: “Aaniin ge-izhi-bimaadiziyang? Wiikaa gego *ji-nitoosiwan*.”**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*Nänabushu was living with his wife and two children. It was there that Nänabushu intended passing the winter, but he had nothing to eat. It was now becoming winter when he undertook to seek for game. Alas! not a thing could he kill. Often was he given the slip by the game-kind, none did he kill. Once he was addressed by his wife saying: “How are we going to live? Never a thing do you kill.”*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Nanabosho a’iindaa wiiwan (Nanabosho was dwelling-there with his wife) ∙ gaye miinawaa gaye i’iw oniijaanisag niizhiwag (and also his two children) ∙ Miisa mii’iw wii-biiboonizhid Nanabosho (and so it was there that Nanabosho was going to spend the winter) ∙ gaawiin gego odayaasiin gaa-miijid (he had nothing to eat) ∙ Miisa mii zhigwa biiboon (and so now it was winter) ∙ maaji-taad andawenjiged (he began to hunt for game) ∙ Ee, gaawesa gego onitoosiin! <span class="l-orig">(2) “Nintawā mōcạg ningakīyōtä,”² i‘kitu Nänabucu. Wayābạnk äjimādcāt. Ningutingidạc, pimusät, ạnicinābän utō‘kawi∙ā∙n māda∙ạ∙nāt; sāga∙i∙ganing tāwạn. Anīnābit utōnta∙i∙bānining, mīdạc imāⁿ wâbạndạnk ma‘kūtiskạ‘k;¹ i∙i´∙wisa twa∙i∙bāgänit i∙i∙māⁿ wänta∙i∙bīnit. Änīci‘kupīt, wīgiwām kīpata‘kitänig. Äjipīndigät ininiwạn nạmạdạpiwạn, kayä i‘kwäwạn, kayä ‘iᵉi´ᵘ unītcānisini nīciwaᵉ. Inābit Nänabucu ma‘kōwīninōn päpa‘kwänit.² Kägä‘t umisawīnawān īni´ᵘ ma‘kuwīninōn.</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**“Nindawaa moozhag ningad-giiyoode*,” ikido Nanabosho. Wayaabang ezhi-maajaad. Ningodingidash, bimosed, anishinaaben oto-kawiin maada’aanaad; zaaga’iganing dawan. Aaniinaabid otoonda’ibaanining, miidash imaan waabandang makotiskaak; i’iwisa dwa’ibaagenid iimaan wenda’ibiinid. Eni-izhi-gobiid, wiigiwaam gibadakidenig. Ezhi-biindiged ininiwan namadabiwan, gaye ikwewan, gaye i’iw oniijaanisini niizhiwag. Inaabid Nanabosho makoowiininoon *baapakwenid*. Geged omisawinawaan iniw makoowiininoon.**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*“Therefore always will I go a-visiting,”² said Nänabushu. On the morrow then he departed. Now once, while walking along, he chanced upon the footprints of some people, in whose path he now followed; by a lake they lived. As he looked about the place from which they drew water, he saw there the anal gut¹ of a bear; now, that was what they used in making a hole from which to obtain water. On going up from the shore, (he saw) a wigwam standing. On entering, (he saw) a man who was seated, also a woman, and their children, numbering two. While looking about, Nänabushu saw (chunks of) bear-tallow, which they used for a lodge-covering.² Truly did he covet the bear-tallow.*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">“Nindawaa moozhag ningad-giiyoode*,” ikido Nanabosho (“So then I will always go visiting,” said Nanabosho) ∙ Wayaabang ezhi-maajaad (on the next day he set out) ∙ Ningodingidash, bimosed (now once, as he was walking) ∙ anishinaaben oto-kawiin maada’aanaad (he followed the trail of some people) ∙ zaaga’iganing dawan (by a lake they dwelt) ∙ Aaniinaabid otoonda’ibaanining (as he looked at their water-hole) ∙ miidash imaan waabandang makotiskaak (and then there he saw a bear’s anal gut) ∙ i’iwisa dwa’ibaagenid iimaan wenda’ibiinid (that was what they used to make the hole where they drew water) ∙ Eni-izhi-gobiid (going up from the shore) ∙ wiigiwaam gibadakidenig (a wigwam stood there) ∙ Ezhi-biindiged ininiwan namadabiwan (on entering, a man sat there) ∙ gaye ikwewan, gaye i’iw oniijaanisini niizhiwag (and a woman, and their two children) ∙ Inaabid Nanabosho makoowiininoon baapakwenid (Nanabosho looked, bear-tallow was used as a covering) ∙ Geged omisawinawaan iniw makoowiininoon (truly he coveted that bear-tallow).</span>

*Jones’s footnote ² (foot of the facing English p.341, its marker on the Ojibwe p.340 at “ningakīyōtä,”²): “Visiting among friends, generally with the sense that the visitor goes to ‘sponge’ off his guests.” — Jones’s footnote ¹ (foot of p.342, keyed to ma‘kūtiskạ‘k;¹): “The anal gut was used to start the hole in the ice by one end of it being held down on the ice, and some one sucking from the other.” — Jones’s footnote ² (foot of p.342, keyed to päpa‘kwänit.²): “The true idea conveyed here is that the strangers had so much food that they could even use it for making a dwelling.”*

<span class="l-orig">(3) Mīdạc ‘iᵉi´ᵘ kīgitōwạn ininiwạn: “Wägunäcina kägīgä∙ạ∙nk³ aᵘ bīwitä?” Mīdạc iᵘ äjikīgitunit īni´ᵘ i‘kwäwạn: “Ānīcînā äntōtạmạn wāwīsiniyạngiban, mī∙i´∙ᵘ kätōtamạmbạn tcigīgä∙i∙wäyạn.”</span>

<span class="l-eng">*And then up spoke the man: “What shall we give the guest (to eat)?”³ Whereat up spoke the woman: “Why, the same as you generally do when we want to eat, is what you should do when providing your gift.”*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Miidash i’iw giigidowan ininiwan (and then the man spoke) ∙ “Wegonezhinaa ge-giige-aank a’aw biiwide?” (“What shall we feed that visitor?”) ∙ Miidash iw ezhi-giigidonid iniw ikwewan (and then the woman spoke) ∙ “Aaniizhinaa endodamaan waa-wiisiniyaangiban” (“In whatever way I usually do when we want to eat”) ∙ “mii’iw ge-doodamambaan ji-giige-iiweyan.” (“that is what you should do when you make your gift.”)</span>

*Jones’s footnote ³ (foot of the facing English p.343, its marker on the Ojibwe p.342 at kägīgä∙ạ∙nk³): “Kägīgä∙ạ∙nk? ‘What shall we give (to eat)?’ This expression occurs in such connections as here, where food is the thing given; and so it has come to be a synonyme for ‘to feed,’ but its real sense is in the giving of a present.”*

<span class="l-orig">(4) Äjikīgitut ‘aᵉa´ᵘ inini: “Tạga, pīdōn ‘iᵉi´ᵘ utcictcini‘k.”⁴ Kägä‘t äcimīnint ‘aᵉa´ᵘ īnini. Kāijimīnint, “‘Ā‘a´ᵘ, unāgạn kāsīyāpi‘kinạn.”</span>

<span class="l-eng">*Truly, when the woman wiped it, thoroughly clean did she make the vessel; next the awl was what she wiped. <span class="l-orig">(7) Ānīc, Nänabucu ā‘pidci pa‘kạdä. “Ạmbägic wäwīp kīgägōwān,” inändạm Nänabucu. <span class="l-eng">*Naturally, Nänabushu was very hungry. “Would that I might presently be given something to eat!” thought Nänabushu. *Jones’s footnote ¹ (foot of p.344, keyed to ugīcōtä∙u∙naᵉ¹): “Ugīcōtä∙u∙naᵉ, ‘mittens;’ the usual word for mittens is mintcikāwanạg. The sense of this word would often seem to imply that it meant ear-warmers or ear-protectors; but it is given as mittens, because the Ojibwa themselves regard that as the sense of the word.” ⚠️ The record carried this note at §3, attributed to p.342, and with its text corrupted: it read “smitten” for the page’s “mittens” TWICE, and mintcikitwanag for mintcikāwanạg. Restored verbatim from the page and re-homed to the section its marker is in. — Jones’s footnote ² (foot of the facing English p.345, its marker on the Ojibwe p.344 at kitickwạntcigạn.”²): “Kitickwạntcigạn, ‘the food which you did not eat up;’ literally, ‘your left-over food.’”*
