29\. NÄNABUSHU, THE SWEET-BRIER BERRIES, AND THE STURGEONS.¹

<span class="l-fiero">**Nenabozho, Oginiig, gaye Namewag.**</span>

Nänabushu, the Sweet-Brier Berries, and the Sturgeons (2)

<span class="l-eng">*Narrated by probably Midāsugaⁿj (Michelson’s own hedge, carried) — Series 2 (Nos. 19–32), Bois Fort (per Michelson’s roster, Part I Preface p. XX; not credited on the story page itself).*</span>

<span class="l-fiero">***from Ojibwa Texts collected by William Jones (1917).***</span>

<span class="l-orig">(1) Weyābạninik äjimādcāt Nänabucu, sāga∙i∙gạnīng odōdisā ‘iᵉi´ᵘ ạnicinābäᵉ; kuniginīn, ininiwạn kayä wīwini; nīciwaᵉ kwīwisänsaᵉ, unīdcānisini. Päjikidạc pa‘kān i‘kwäwạn ningutci imā tāwạn, ā‘pidcisa usāgī∙i∙gōn īni´ᵘ ininiwạn. “Kägä‘tsa, Nänabucu, ambäsa, wīwin imā aᵘ i‘kwä.”</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**Weyaabaninig e-zhi-maajaad Nenabozho, zaaga’iganing odoodisaan i’iw anishinaaben; gonowaa, ininiwan gaye wiiwini; niizhiwe’ gwiiwizhensag, oniijaanisini. Bezhigidash bakaan ikwewan ningoji imaa daawan, e-bizhishig osaagi’igoon iniw ininiwan. “Gegetsa, Nenabozho, ambe, wiiwin imaa a’aw ikwe.”**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*When the morrow came, then off started Nänabushu; at a lake he came upon some people, behold, a man and his wife; there were two boys, their children. And there was one other woman who lived in another place, very much was she loved by the man. “To be sure, Nänabushu, come, do you take to wife the woman yonder.”*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Weyaabaninig e-zhi-maajaad Nenabozho (when morning came, off Nenabozho went) ∙ zaaga’iganing odoodisaan i’iw anishinaaben (at a lake he reached the people) ∙ gonowaa, ininiwan gaye wiiwini (behold, a man and his wife) ∙ niizhiwe’ gwiiwizhensag, oniijaanisini (two boys, their children) ∙ bezhigidash bakaan ikwewan ningoji imaa daawan (and one other woman dwelt somewhere there) ∙ e-bizhishig osaagi’igoon iniw ininiwan (greatly was she loved by the man) ∙ “Gegetsa, Nenabozho, ambe, wiiwin imaa a’aw ikwe” (“indeed, Nenabozho, come, take that woman to wife”).</span>

<span class="l-orig">(5) Ānīc, mī∙i´∙ᵘ cigwa ạnitạgwāgininig; kayädạc päpa‘kān tāwạg, ānīc mamawadisitiwạg īni´ᵘ ininiwạn. “Mīsa cigwa tcimādci‘taiyạnk ‘iᵉiwä tcinōtcigīⁿgōⁿ∙i∙wäyạnk.” Ānīc kägä‘t äjinōtcigīⁿgōⁿ∙i∙wäwāt, adi‘kạmägwaᵉ ki‘tcinībiwa unisāwaᵉ. Ānīc ạdcidạgōnā‘ku‘käwạg. Ā‘pidcisa nībiwa unisāwaᵉ.</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**Aaniish, mii’iw zhigwa ani-dagwaagininig; gayedash bakaan daawag, aaniish mamawi-daawag iniw ininiwan. “Misa zhigwa ji-maajii-itaayang i’iwa ji-noojigiigoonyiwewang.” Aaniish geget e-zhi-noojigiigoonyiwewaad, adikamegweg gichi-niibiwa onisaaweg. Aaniish ajidagonaa’ko-gaawag. E-bizhishig niibiwa onisaaweg.**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*Now, by this time the autumn was come; and they dwelt apart from (the other family), but they visited back and forth with the man. “It is now time for us to set to work getting fish.” Now, to be sure, when they went to get fish, whitefish in great plenty they killed. Now they made a rack to hang them with head down. Ever so many they killed.*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Aaniish, mii’iw zhigwa ani-dagwaagininig (now, by then the fall was coming on) ∙ gayedash bakaan daawag (and apart they dwelt) ∙ aaniish mamawi-daawag iniw ininiwan (yet they kept company with the man) ∙ “Misa zhigwa ji-maajii-itaayang i’iwa ji-noojigiigoonyiwewang” (“now it is time we set to fishing”) ∙ aaniish geget e-zhi-noojigiigoonyiwewaad (so truly when they fished) ∙ adikamegweg gichi-niibiwa onisaaweg (whitefish in great plenty they took) ∙ ajidagonaa’ko-gaawag (they made a rack to hang them head-down) ∙ e-bizhishig niibiwa onisaaweg (very many they killed).</span>

<span class="l-orig">(6) Ningutingigu äcikackạtininig ‘iᵉi´mā ändạcikīⁿgōⁿ∙i∙käwāt; mīsā‘pạn kīkackạtininig ‘iᵉi´ᵘ sāga∙i∙gạn. Ạbāpic kākackạtininig, “Ạmbäsạnō,” i‘kito Nänabucu: “intawānā kīni‘tạmawā kīga∙ạ∙mwānānig īgiwä kīdạtcitạgōtākaniminānig.”</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**Ningoding igo e-zhi-gashkadininig i’iw endazhi-giigoonyi-gaawaad; misa’pan gashkadininig i’iw zaaga’igan. A’pii gaa-gashkadininig, “Ambesanoo,” ikido Nenabozho: “indawaa giin’tamawaa giga-amwaanaanig igiw gidaji-jaagootaakaniminaanig.”**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*And once after it had frozen over there where they were catching the fish; for the lake was now frozen. When it was frozen over, “Now then,” said Nänabushu: “let us first eat up these (fishes) of yours which we have hung up with head down.”*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Ningoding igo e-zhi-gashkadininig (and once when it froze over) ∙ i’iw endazhi-giigoonyi-gaawaad (where they had been taking fish) ∙ misa’pan gashkadininig i’iw zaaga’igan (so frozen was the lake) ∙ “Ambesanoo,” ikido Nenabozho (“now then,” said Nenabozho) ∙ “indawaa giin’tamawaa giga-amwaanaanig igiw (“let us first eat up those) ∙ gidaji-jaagootaakaniminaanig” (we have hung up to dry”).</span>

<span class="l-orig">(7) Ānīc, mīgu iᵘ gägä‘t äcictcigäwāt. Kumägu ä‘pītcibibōninig äjigitạmāwāt, mīdạc ä‘ta wīnawā ugīⁿgōⁿ∙i∙miwā. Cigwasa mādcā ‘aᵉa´ᵘ īnini; änitạgwicing wänāgucininig; ä‘tawā, kuniginīn kā∙u∙ndcikusinigwän. Mīdạc awinini äcigạnōnāt īni´ᵘ wīwạn: “Mīmāwīni iᵘ kạnabạtc tcikitcinōntāyābạniciyạng. Intawāsa mīnōtc ningakīyusä.” Ānīc kägä‘t äjikīyusät, kāwīnsa kägō uni‘tōsīn. Kägä‘t sạnạgạtini; wī‘kā‘ku päjik pinäwạn upīnān. Käga‘pīgu pa‘kadäwạg. Ningutingigu kāwīn kanạgä ōpīnāsīn īni´ᵘ pinäwạn. Mīsa´ gägä‘t äjipa‘kadäwāt. Intawādạc uginiᵉ unạntunäwāwān. Mīsạnā ‘iᵉi´ᵘ uginiᵉ äjiạmwāwāt, wänkitci cigu kawạnāntạmōg.</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**Aaniish, mii go iw geget e-zhichigewaad. Gomaago e-bizhishigi-biibooninig e-zhi-gidamaawaad, miidash eta wiinawaa ogiigoonyimiwaa. Zhigwasa maajaa a’aw inini; ani-dagwishing wenaagoshininig; e-tawaa, gonowaa gaa-oonji-goozinigwen. Miidash a’aw inini e-zhi-ganoonaad iniw wiiwan: “Miimaawiini iw ganabaj ji-gichi-noondaayaabanizhiyang. Indawaasa miinooch ninga-giiyose.” Aaniish geget e-zhi-giiyosed, gaawiinsa gegoo oni’toosiin. Geget zanagadini; wiikaa go bezhig binewan obiinaan. Ningoding igo gaawiin ganage obiinaasiin iniw binewan. Misa’ geget e-zhi-ba’kadewaad. Indawaadash oginiin onandawewaawaan. Miisana i’iw oginiin e-zhi-amwaawaad, wenji-go gawanaandamoog.**</span>

<span class="l-eng">*Well, that was what they truly did. And later in the winter when they ate them all up, there still remained the fish (of Nänabushu and his wife). Then off went the man; he arrived at where he went in the evening; alas! he found that they must have moved camp. Thereupon the man spoke to his wife, saying: “There is a doubtful chance if we shall be able to live through the winter. Therefore on that account I will hunt for game.” So truly when he hunted, he could not kill anything. To be sure, it was a trying time; once in a long while he fetched home only a single ruffed grouse. And at last not even a single ruffed grouse did he bring home. Thereupon they were truly hungry. So on that account for sweet-brier berries they went to seek. Although they ate the sweet-brier berries, yet by degrees were they starving.*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Aaniish, mii go iw geget e-zhichigewaad (well, that truly they did) ∙ gomaago e-bizhishigi-biibooninig e-zhi-gidamaawaad (later in deep winter when they ate them up) ∙ miidash eta wiinawaa ogiigoonyimiwaa (then only theirs, their fish, remained) ∙ zhigwasa maajaa a’aw inini (then off went the man) ∙ ani-dagwishing wenaagoshininig (he arrived as evening came) ∙ gonowaa gaa-oonji-goozinigwen (behold, they must have moved camp) ∙ e-zhi-ganoonaad iniw wiiwan (he spoke to his wife) ∙ “ganabaj ji-gichi-noondaayaabanizhiyang” (“a doubtful chance we live through the winter”) ∙ “Indawaasa miinooch ninga-giiyose” (“so even so I will hunt”) ∙ gaawiinsa gegoo oni’toosiin (yet nothing did he kill) ∙ wiikaa go bezhig binewan obiinaan (once in a long while one grouse he brought) ∙ gaawiin ganage obiinaasiin (not even one did he bring) ∙ misa’ geget e-zhi-ba’kadewaad (so truly they hungered) ∙ oginiin onandawewaawaan (sweet-brier berries they sought) ∙ wenji-go gawanaandamoog (yet they were starving).</span>

<span class="l-fiero">**Ningoding idash miinawaa e-zhi-maajaad babaa-nandawiiginiiwaad e-bizhishig-igo gisinaani gayedash-igo gii’kaj. Zaaga’iganing e-zhi-madaabiid. <span class="l-eng">*And another time when he set out to seek for sweet-brier berries, the weather was very cold and he was shivering. Out upon a lake he came. And so when he went along yonder sunny side, in abundance he found the sweet-brier berries. Now, into a miserable cloth bag he put them. And by and by, while going along the shore, he suddenly heard the sound of something fall yonder on the ice. When he looked, something was there. Thereupon he went out upon the ice, going up to where it was; when he got a near view of it, lo, (it was) an arrow feathered with the ear of a bear! As he started to pick (the arrow) up to examine it, all at once by some one was he addressed: “Hold, hold! Is the arrow yours that you are picking up?”*</span>

<span class="l-gloss">Ningoding idash miinawaa e-zhi-maajaad babaa-nandawiiginiiwaad (another time off he went seeking sweet-brier berries) ∙ e-bizhishig-igo gisinaani gayedash gii’kaj (the weather very cold and he shivering) ∙ zaaga’iganing e-zhi-madaabiid (out upon a lake he came) ∙ enaasamaa-daawaning ani-izhi-ayaad (going along the sunny side) ∙ e-bizhishig omii’kawaan i’iw oginiin (in plenty he found the berries) ∙ babagiwayaane-ashkimodaazhing odanibinaawan (into a poor cloth bag he put them) ∙ ani-bimaajagaamed (going along the shore) ∙ gegoo noondang madwesininig imaa mi’kwaming (he heard something fall on the ice) ∙ gonowaa bikwa’k ma’ko-dawagan asawewing (behold, an arrow feathered with a bear’s ear) ∙ e-zhi-odaa’pinang wiiwaawaanaabandang (as he took it up to look at it) ∙ baamaa-go awiya wenji-ganoonigod (suddenly someone spoke to him) ∙ “Daawa’t, daawa’t!
